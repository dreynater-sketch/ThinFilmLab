# Superconducting critical temperatures, critical magnetic fields, lattice parameters, and chemical compositions of ‘‘bulk’’ pure and alloyed Nb3Sn produced by the bronze process

**Suenaga et al. (1986)** — Journal of Applied Physics

DOI: 10.1063/1.336607

*Zotero collections: Materials Science & Engineering > Metallurgy & Phase Diagrams*

---

## Abstract

Superconducting critical temperatures Tc and magnetic fields Hc2, lattice parameters a0, and chemical compositions were measured for ‘‘bulk’’ layers (∼6 μm or greater) of ‘‘pure’’ and alloyed Nb3Sn which were made by the bronze process. The values of Tc, a0, and the composition of pure Nb3Sn layers were ∼18 K, 0.52900±0.00005 nm, and 25±0.5 at. % Sn, respectively, independent of heat-treatment temperature (between 650–780 °C) and of the bronze composition, as long as the thickness of the layers was greater than ∼6 μm. Small additions of Ti (∼1 at. %) or Ta (∼3 at. %) slightly increased the value of Tc (by ∼0.2–0.4 K) above that for pure Nb3Sn. However, additions of larger amounts of these elements or addition of other transition elements (V, Zr, and Mo) significantly decreased Tc. Also, small additions of these elements significantly increased Hc2. Specifically, the largest values of Hc2 (∼27 T at 4.2 K) were obtained for Nb3Sn layers containing ∼1.5 and ∼4 at. % of Ti and Ta, respectively, compared with a value for the pure Nb3Sn of 23.5 T at 4.2 K. The value of a0 decreased with all of the alloying additions; these variations can be explained qualitatively by several models for the lattice parameter of A15 compounds, but none of them can quantitatively predict the variations. In one system, (Nb,Ti)3Sn, values of the normal-state resistivity just above the transition temperature were measured: adding 3 at. % Ti raises the value to ∼55 μΩ cm from the value of 10–15 μΩ cm for pure Nb3Sn. This increase in the resistivity is thought to be a primary reason for the increased Hc2 for this system as well as the other types of alloyed Nb3Sn which were studied here.

## Summary

Foundational metallurgical characterization of bulk (≥6 µm) bronze-process Nb3Sn — pure and alloyed with Ti, Ta, V, Zr, Mo. Brookhaven, 1986. Establishes the baseline properties of "good" Nb3Sn (Tc, a₀, stoichiometry) and how dilute alloying shifts Tc, Hc2, and lattice parameter. **The result most relevant to current grain-boundary work: the lattice-parameter analysis independently confirms that Cu sits at the grain boundaries, not in the A15 lattice** — the classic citation for Cu's near-zero solubility in Nb3Sn. Also the canonical demonstration that small Ti (~1 at%) or Ta (~3 at%) additions *raise* Tc slightly and boost Hc2, via increased normal-state resistivity (electron scattering), while larger additions degrade Tc.

## Key Findings

### 1. "Good" bronze-process Nb3Sn is robust and stoichiometric
For layers thicker than ~6 µm, **independent of heat-treatment T (650–780 °C) and bronze Sn content (3.5–7.5 at%)**:
- Tc ≈ **18 K** (measured without matrix, i.e. strain-free)
- a₀ = **0.52900 ± 0.00005 nm**
- composition = **25 ± 0.5 at% Sn** (stoichiometric)

Thin layers show lower Tc / more strain sensitivity (disorder + matrix strain). Even a low-Sn bronze (3.5 at%) yields "good" Nb3Sn if reacted long enough to grow a thick layer.

### 2. Cu is at the grain boundaries, NOT in the A15 lattice ★
- Chemical analysis: Nb content had to be **renormalized by removing Cu** — Cu "is primarily at the grain boundaries of the Nb3Sn layers, rather than incorporated in the A15 lattice."
- **Independent confirmation via lattice parameter**: hard-sphere models (Geller, Pauling, Pearson) reproduce the measured Δa₀/a₀ vs alloying **only when Cu is excluded from the lattice** (assumed to be in GBs). Including Cu in the lattice destroys the consistency. → strong, model-independent evidence that Cu segregates to boundaries.

### 3. Ti and Ta: small additions raise Tc; all raise Hc2
- Small **Ti (~1 at%)** or **Ta (~3 at%)** raise Tc by ~0.2–0.4 K above pure (max ~18.1–18.5 K). Larger Ti/Ta, or any V/Zr/Mo, **decrease** Tc.
- **Hc2(4.2 K)**: pure = 23.5 T; max **~27 T** at ~1.5 at% Ti or ~4 at% Ta.
- **Mechanism of higher Hc2 = higher normal-state resistivity** (more electron scattering). Adding 3 at% Ti raises ρ to ~55 µΩ·cm from 10–15 µΩ·cm for pure Nb3Sn.
- The small Tc *bump* is a delicate balance: alloying lowers N(0) (↓Tc) but lowering the martensitic transformation temperature T_M softens phonons (↑Tc); peak Tc occurs where T_M ≈ Tc.

### 4. Lattice parameter and site occupancy
- a₀ decreases with all alloying additions; rate per at%: Ta < Ti < V < Mo < Zr.
- Ti and Ta show **anomalously large effective radii** → likely **antisite disorder** accompanies their alloying.
- All solutes (except possibly Zr) occupy **Nb (A) sites**; Zr may occupy both.
- Ti most strongly reduces Sn stoichiometry; Zr reduces Sn most per at% and is barely incorporated (lost as ZrO2).

## Methods Used

- Bronze-process monofilament wires (Nb or Nb-alloy core in Cu–Sn bronze), reacted 650–780 °C, matrix etched off before measurement (strain-free Tc).
- **Tc**: inductive, ±0.05 K. **Hc2(4.2 K)**: resistive, hybrid magnet to 30 T (Francis Bitter NML). **a₀**: Debye–Scherrer XRD, Nelson–Riley extrapolation. **Composition**: SEM-WDS with ZAF. **ρ**: normal-state resistivity above Tc.
- Lattice-parameter modeling: modified Geller, Pauling, Pearson hard-sphere models (Appendix).

## Open Questions

- Suenaga's Cu-at-GB conclusion is for **bulk wire** layers; does FSU's thin bronze film show the same Cu exclusion from the lattice, or do thinner films / lower thermal budget trap more Cu in the A15? (APT/EDS needed.)
- The Hc2 gain comes from resistivity (disorder). For FSU's *SRF* application the goal is the opposite (low surface resistance) — so the wire-optimization logic (deliberately add scattering for Hc2) does **not** transfer to cavities. Worth keeping straight when citing this for an SRF paper.
