## Field 1 - Academic & Professional Background

*Prompt: Tell us about yourself, including your educational and work background in detail in 4-5+ sentences. If you have any special skills that may be relevant, please let us know about those too. The more detail, the better!*

I'm Andre Juliao. I finished my PhD in experimental condensed matter physics at Florida State University in November 2025 and now lead the Nb3Sn thin-film program at the Applied Superconductivity Center / NHMFL at FSU, under Prof. Lance Cooley. The work is Nb3Sn coatings on copper for two applications: SRF accelerator cavities, and axion dark-matter haloscope cavities that operate at millikelvin temperatures in fields up to 9 T.

In the lab I run the full deposition and characterization chain. Deposition side: DC magnetron sputtering of Ta / Cu-Sn / Nb multilayers on our Kurt J. Lesker 5-target system, thermal evaporation of Sn on an Edwards Auto 306, and vacuum heat treatments to form the A15 phase. Characterization side: SQUID/VSM for Tc and M(H), XRD for phase ID, SEM/EDS and FIB cross-sectioning on a FEI Helios G4, and PPMS RF quality-factor measurements against a VNA in fields up to 9 T. I have one paper published in Superconductor Science & Technology (Withanage, Juliao, Cooley 2021), a second SST manuscript under review on a diffusion-bonded Cu-Sn seam verified by magneto-optical imaging, and three more manuscripts in preparation.

A large part of my week is spent mentoring two student researchers through structured deposition and characterization tracks, so I'm very used to explaining physics at different levels, from BCS and Ginzburg-Landau at the derivation level up through RF surface resistance, vortex dynamics (Gittleman-Rosenblum), and thin-film microstructure.

Technical skills relevant to this role: fluent with LaTeX, Python (NumPy, SciPy, Matplotlib), MATLAB, Mathematica, and ImageJ. Comfortable across SI, Gaussian, and natural units, with error propagation, and with critical reading of technical papers at the level of peer review. I keep a personal Zotero library of about 500 superconductivity and accelerator-physics papers that I use to check claims against primary sources.

## Field 2 - Physics Courses, Favorite & Why

*Prompt: Tell us about the physics courses you specialized in during your studies. Which one was your favorite and why? We'd love to hear about any topics, projects, or challenges that stood out to you!*

My graduate coursework was the standard condensed-matter track: graduate quantum mechanics (Sakurai-level), graduate electrodynamics (Jackson), graduate statistical mechanics (Pathria), solid state (Ashcroft and Mermin), and quantum field theory / many-body theory. On top of that I took specialized courses on superconductivity and on accelerator physics, plus USPAS material on SRF fundamentals.

My favorite was the superconductivity sequence. It's the one subject where all the earlier machinery gets used at the same time: second quantization for BCS, Jackson-level E&M for the London equations and the Mattis-Bardeen surface impedance, stat mech for Ginzburg-Landau and the vortex lattice, and solid state for the electronic structure and phonon coupling that make an A15 compound like Nb3Sn superconducting in the first place.

What I found interesting, and what drove my PhD, was how badly the clean textbook picture holds up once you look at real films. Tc of Nb3Sn drops off fast once the Sn content goes below about 24 at.%. Thermal-expansion mismatch between copper and Nb3Sn puts the film under roughly -1.24% biaxial strain on cooldown, which lowers the flux-entry field on Cu-based films to around 11 mT. Grain boundaries and residual Cu inclusions dominate pinning in ways no analytic model handles cleanly.

The thesis problem I kept chasing was depositing Nb3Sn on copper at 650-750 °C, which is below Cu's melting point and well below the 1100 °C Sn-vapor route, and still getting columnar Zone-2 Thornton grains instead of a disordered polycrystal. That ended up being the place where thin-film growth, the Cu-Sn / Nb-Sn / Nb-Sn-Cu phase diagrams, and superconducting transport physics all had to line up at once, and that's what kept it interesting to me.
